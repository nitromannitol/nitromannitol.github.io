Site percolation at criticality

In Bernoulli site percolation on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\), each site is open with probability \(p\) and closed with probability \(1-p\), independently of the other sites. The open clusters are the connected components of the open sites, and \(\theta(p)\) is the probability that the origin belongs to an infinite cluster.

Increasing \(p\) can only enlarge open clusters, so \(\theta\) is nondecreasing. There is a critical probability \(p_c\) with \(\theta(p)=0\) for \(p<p_c\) and \(\theta(p)>0\) for \(p>p_c\). The endpoint question is whether \(\theta(p_c)=0\).

In two dimensions the answer is classical. For bond percolation it follows from two theorems: Harris proved that the percolation probability vanishes at \(p=1/2\), and Kesten proved that \(1/2\) is the critical probability. For site percolation it is Russo, who used Kesten's identification of the bond threshold to obtain continuity of \(\theta\) on \([0,1]\). The checked site theorem described on this page is a separate declaration for \(d\geq3\); it does not include the classical \(d=2\) case.

For bond percolation in high dimensions, the lace expansion gives the result for \(d \ge 19\) by Hara and Slade and then for \(d \ge 11\) by Fitzner and van der Hofstad. A 2026 paper by Cerf discusses the site-percolation endpoint problem for every \(d\geq3\) and the relevance of the Kozma–Nitzan gluing program.

Anthropic's Lean development proves a sufficient additive gluing estimate for finite weighted graphs, derives the corresponding near-one bond gluing statement, and uses the Kozma–Nitzan reduction to prove the bond endpoint theorem. The present development builds on that library. Its distinct finite result is the multiplicative labeled-hyperedge inequality displayed below, which supplies the site-percolation gluing input and leads to the checked theorem for \(d\geq3\). The companion page treats the remaining conjectures and questions of the Kozma and Nitzan paper.

Simulations

Finite hyperedge model and gluing inequality

Bond and site percolation admit useful transformations, but a connection inequality in one formulation does not automatically transfer with the same events and probabilities to the other. Fisher's line-graph construction is a standard relation between the models, while Gladkov and Zimin identify limitations on approximating general site models by bond models. The formalization therefore works in a finite incidence structure that has both bond and site specializations.

The common model has a finite set of sites and a finite set of labels. Each label is attached to a set of sites and is open independently. Two sites are connected when a chain of open labels joins them.

When every label is attached to two sites, the labels act as bond variables. A separate incidence construction uses independent labels as site variables and preserves the site-connectivity events needed in the proof. These are the two specializations used below.

The finite theorem states that for a nonempty set \(A\) of sites and sites \(o\) and \(b\), \[ \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow b,\ o \leftrightarrow A) \;\ge\; \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow A)\,\min_{a \in A}\mathbb{P}(a \leftrightarrow b). \tag{1} \] The right side combines the probability of reaching \(A\) with the smallest connection probability from a member of \(A\) to \(b\). Kozma and Nitzan conjectured the corresponding multiplicative statement for a finite weighted graph, the specialization in which labels are edges: \[ \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow b) \;\ge\; \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow A)\,\min_{a \in A}\mathbb{P}(a \leftrightarrow b). \tag{2} \] Inequality (1) allows labels incident to more than two sites and retains \(\{o \leftrightarrow A\}\) on the left. Discarding that event gives (2) in the bond specialization; the site specialization gives its site counterpart.

This multiplicative hyperedge theorem is not the same statement as Anthropic's additive bond theorem. The latter says that if every \(a\in A\) reaches \(b\) with failure probability at most \(t\), then \(\mathbb{P}(o\leftrightarrow b)\geq\mathbb{P}(o\leftrightarrow A)-t\); it yields the near-one conclusion needed by the Kozma–Nitzan reduction. Inequality (1) is the finite theorem proved in this development. It avoids the factor of \(|A|\) that a direct union bound would introduce.

Finite hyperedge gluing

The proof uses finite product measures, conditional correlation inequalities, and finite combinatorics. The notes give a longer account.

Correlation under conditioning

The starting point is Harris's inequality: two events that are both made easier by opening labels occur together at least as often as independence would give. It is proved by induction on the number of labels, each step a rearrangement inequality on one coordinate. A more flexible form is the four functions theorem of Ahlswede and Daykin: if four nonnegative functions of a set satisfy \[ f_1(I)\,f_2(J) \;\le\; f_3(I \cup J)\,f_4(I \cap J) \] for every pair \(I,J\), then the same inequality holds for their sums over all sets. This transfers pointwise bounds to sums over configurations.

The conditioning needed for (1) can destroy positive association. On the path \(x - z - y\) with both edges open with probability \(1/2\), conditioned on \(x\) not reaching \(y\), the two edges are open with conditional probability \(1/3\) each and never together. The two increasing events are negatively correlated under this conditioning.

Exposure of independent labels

A more specific positive-correlation statement does hold for increasing functions of the cluster of the conditioned site. For graphs this is the theorem of van den Berg, Häggström and Kahn; here it is proved for the general structure. The proof is an induction on the number of sites in which a set \(Z\) is fixed, the labels meeting \(Z\) are exposed, and the four functions theorem is applied on the lattice of subsets of the exposed labels.

The induction must retain the exposed labels rather than only their vertex traces. Write \(T(I)\) for the set of sites outside \(Z\) that the open exposed labels \(I\) reach. Some graph arguments index boundary exposures by the reached vertices. That compression is unavailable for general labels: two labels attached to the same sites have the same trace, and so do a label reaching \(\{a,b\}\) and one reaching \(\{b,c\}\) once \(b\) has been exposed. The trace maps satisfy \[ T(I \cup J) = T(I) \cup T(J), \qquad T(I \cap J) \subseteq T(I) \cap T(J), \] and the second inclusion is strict in both examples. The induction is therefore indexed by the labels themselves. The reason is the measure rather than the four-functions step: the structure with a cluster deleted carries a product law because distinct labels stay distinct, and a record keeping only the sites a label reaches would identify two labels that reach the same sites.

Conditional covariance inequalities

The argument also needs a comparison between the values of one conditional covariance at two different sites. Fix a site \(x\), a set \(Y\) its cluster must avoid, and an increasing function \(f\) of the labels of that cluster, and write \[ \kappa(u) \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(f,\ \mathbf{1}\{u \leftrightarrow x\} \ \big|\ x \nleftrightarrow Y \bigr). \] The theorem is that for two further sites \(o\) and \(v\) and any finite list \(d_1,\dots,d_\ell\) of others, a certain alternating sum, in which the contribution of each \(d_j\) is subtracted off in turn, satisfies \[ \kappa^{[\ell]}(o) \;\ge\; \lambda\,\kappa^{[\ell]}(v), \] where \(\lambda\) is a connection probability conditional on avoiding the designated set. With \(Y\) empty, no \(d_j\), and \(f\) the indicator that \(x\) reaches \(v\), this is Harris's inequality. These inequalities handle both conditioning and the successive subtraction of other sites' contributions. The latter property closes the induction and accounts for retaining the sites \(d_j\).

Its proof is a strong induction on \(\ell\) in three steps. The cluster of \(Y\) is exposed first, which turns the conditional covariance into an average of unconditional covariances over the structures that survive, and a telescoping argument reduces the theorem to the nonnegativity of that average. The sites \(d_j\) are then removed one at a time by an exact identity, and the error each removal makes is itself a conditional covariance with a shorter list, so the induction hypothesis applies to it. The remaining term compares \(o\) with \(v\) inside a single surviving structure, and its local factor averages to \(\lambda\). The last two steps use inequalities about two clusters at once, including a form of Harris's inequality valid at a stopping time of an exploration.

Induction on the intermediate set

Inequality (1) follows from the conditional covariance inequalities by induction on the size of \(A\). The proof assigns the comparison to a single site of \(A\) and to no other: order the sites of \(A\) by the conditional mean of \(f\), and in each configuration charge the one of least rank that the source actually reaches. Every configuration in which the source reaches \(A\) names exactly one such site, so the events partition rather than accumulate. No term is summed over all of \(A\), so the estimate does not degrade as \(A\) grows.

Block renormalization at a smaller parameter

The passage to \(\mathbb{Z}^d\) is a renormalization. Partition the lattice into blocks of a fixed size, declare a block occupied when a prescribed connection occurs inside it, and compare the occupied blocks with site percolation on a coarse lattice whose sites are the blocks. If the occupied blocks percolate on the coarse lattice, the open structure percolates on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\).

The construction requires one estimate: a block already known to be occupied is joined to a neighboring block with conditional probability close to one, given the revealed history.

The Grimmett–Marstrand slab theorem obtains that estimate by sprinkling. It opens further sites, working at \(p+\gamma\) rather than at \(p\), and conclude that percolation at \(p\) forces percolation in a slab of finite width at \(p+\gamma\), for every \(\gamma>0\). Their argument requires \(\gamma>0\); at \(p=p_c\), the resulting slab parameter \(p_c+\gamma\) is above criticality.

Inequality (1) gives the estimate without sprinkling. Condition on the configuration in a shell around the block. That fixes the states on the shell and exposes a set \(A\) of open sites there, each of which reaches the neighboring block with conditional probability at least \(1-\delta\). Take \(o\) to be the source and \(b\) the target in the neighboring block: the two factors on the right of (1) are the probability that \(o\) reaches \(A\), which the previous step made close to one, and the minimum over \(A\), which is at least \(1-\delta\). These conditional bounds are uniform over the revealed exploration history. The occupied blocks dominate independent site percolation at density \(1-\rho\) on the coarse lattice, and \(\rho\) is chosen small enough for that to percolate.

Every probability used in the construction concerns finitely many sites and is strictly above its threshold at \(p\), so the same finite list of inequalities still holds at some \(q<p\). Rerunning the construction at \(q\) gives \(\theta(q)>0\). Applied at \(p=p_c\) this contradicts the definition of \(p_c\), and the conclusion is \(\theta(p_c)=0\).

The checked endpoint extends the planar block construction in a third coordinate and therefore assumes \(d\geq3\). The thin region \(\mathbb{Z}^2 \times \{0,\dots,k\}^{d-2}\) reduces to the plane when \(d=2\), but the present construction does not specialize to that case. The planar site theorem is classical and lies outside this Lean declaration.

Lean formalization

The Lean development proves the finite inequality (1) and the site-percolation theorem described here. This page summarizes their mathematical context; it does not follow the source files declaration by declaration.

The finite incidence structure, connection events, percolation probability, and critical parameter are defined in the files. The critical parameter is proved to lie in \([0,1]\), so \(\theta(p_c)\) is defined without an additional side condition.

The development also proves exact factorization after conditioning on the whole cluster of a set, factorization of two disjoint clusters in either order, and expansion of a probability as a finite sum over the possible clusters. The percolation probability is monotone in the parameter and in the underlying structure, vanishes below \(p_c\), and \(0<p_c<1\). An event depending on \(n\) sites has probability Lipschitz in the parameter with constant \(n\). An exploration whose every step succeeds with probability at least \(a\), whatever the history, reaches an upward-closed target at least as often as an independent Bernoulli \(a\) sequence. The example showing that the trace of an intersection can be strictly smaller than the intersection of the traces is also proved in Lean.

Verified statements

Inequality (1) has a Lean proof for arbitrary finite independent labeled-hyperedge models. The site theorem also has a Lean proof. In an isolated copy, every local Lean file needed by the site theorem was compiled again from source. A separate check reported the public statement

∀ (d : ℕ), 3 ≤ d → thetaSite d (criticalProbSiteI d) = 0

Its only mathematical assumption is \(3\leq d\). Lean lists exactly the axioms propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. The Lean files used in its proof contain no sorry, admit, native_decide, or added axiom.

This checked site declaration covers exactly \(d\geq3\). The site result for \(d=2\) is classical, but is not part of this declaration; as noted above, the current endpoint proof extends the block construction in a third coordinate. Anthropic's separate bond-percolation theorem covers \(d\geq2\) through its additive and near-one gluing route.
