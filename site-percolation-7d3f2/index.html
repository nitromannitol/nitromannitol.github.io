Site percolation at criticality

ChatGPT 5.6 Sol and Claude Fable 5.1, prompted by Ahmed Bou-Rabee

Last updated 3 September 2026, 4:21 PM EDT

In Bernoulli site percolation on \(\mathbb{Z}^d\) each site is open with probability \(p\) and closed with probability \(1-p\), independently of every other site. The open clusters are the connected components of the set of open sites, and \(\theta(p)\) is the probability that the origin lies in an infinite one.

Raising \(p\) opens more sites and can only enlarge open clusters, so \(\theta\) is nondecreasing. There is therefore a critical probability \(p_c\) with \(\theta(p)=0\) for \(p<p_c\) and \(\theta(p)>0\) for \(p>p_c\). Does \(\theta(p_c)=0\)?

In two dimensions the answer is yes. For bond percolation, in which edges rather than sites are open, it is the combination of two theorems: Harris proved that the percolation probability vanishes at \(p = 1/2\), and Kesten proved that \(1/2\) is the critical probability. The site case is Russo, who used Kesten's identification of the bond threshold to obtain continuity of \(\theta\) on all of \([0,1]\). In three or more dimensions the answer is yes for bond percolation, by Anthropic's machine-checked proof of Conjecture 3 of Kozma and Nitzan. That settled the dimensions three to ten, the only ones still open: the lace expansion had already given the result in high dimensions, for \(d \ge 19\) by Hara and Slade and then for \(d \ge 11\) by Fitzner and van der Hofstad. For site percolation in three or more dimensions it is not known in general, and that is the case treated here. The companion page treats the remaining conjectures and questions of the Kozma and Nitzan paper.

Simulations

A common generalization

It is classical that site percolation is the more general of the two models. Fisher observed that bond percolation on a graph is site percolation on the graph whose vertices are its edges, two of them adjacent when they share an endpoint. The converse fails: Gladkov and Zimin proved that bond percolation cannot simulate site percolation even approximately, already near a vertex of degree three. The site inequality therefore cannot be deduced from the bond one, and a statement covering both models must be proved in a structure containing both.

One such structure is the following. Take a finite set of sites together with a finite set of labels, attach to each label a collection of sites, and declare each label open independently of the others. Two sites are connected when a chain of open labels joins them, consecutive labels in the chain sharing a site.

Attaching every label to exactly two sites makes the labels edges, and the structure is bond percolation. Giving each site a label of its own, attached to that site and to one further point for each edge at it, makes a label open exactly when its site is open, and the structure is site percolation. In the second case the labels are the sites, so the two models have the same sample space and the same probabilities, and a chain of open labels read at every second step is a path of open sites.

The inequality at issue is the following. For a nonempty set \(A\) of sites and sites \(o\) and \(b\), \[ \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow b,\ o \leftrightarrow A) \;\ge\; \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow A)\,\min_{a \in A}\mathbb{P}(a \leftrightarrow b). \] Read from the right, it says that reaching \(A\) and then continuing to \(b\) from the least favourable point of \(A\) is no better than reaching \(b\) directly. For labels attached to two sites, and after dropping the second event on the left, it is the inequality Kozma and Nitzan conjectured. The general form implies the bond inequality and its site counterpart together, so one finite theorem serves both models.

The exposure record

The induction behind the inequality exposes the labels incident to a set \(Z\) and records what those labels reach outside \(Z\). Write \(T(I)\) for the set of sites outside \(Z\) reached by a set \(I\) of labels.

For graphs, \(I\) can be recovered from \(T(I)\). A label incident to two sites, one of them in \(Z\), is named by the other. For labels incident to more than two sites this fails. Two labels incident to the same sites have the same trace, and so do a label reaching \(\{a,b\}\) and one reaching \(\{b,c\}\) once \(b\) has been exposed.

The consequence is an asymmetry, \[ T(I \cup J) = T(I) \cup T(J), \qquad T(I \cap J) \subseteq T(I) \cap T(J), \] with the second inclusion strict in both examples. The induction applies the four functions theorem to \(T(I)\), \(T(J)\), \(T(I \cap J)\) and \(T(I \cup J)\), and so needs both relations to be equalities. Recording \(I\) itself restores them, since unions and intersections of label sets are unions and intersections.

Renormalization

The lattice argument is a renormalization. Partition \(\mathbb{Z}^d\) into blocks, call a block occupied when a prescribed connection occurs inside it, and compare the occupied blocks with site percolation on a coarser lattice. The step to be justified is that an occupied block is joined to its neighbour with probability close to one, conditionally on everything already examined.

Grimmett and Marstrand justify it by sprinkling. They open further sites, at parameter \(p + \gamma\), and obtain percolation in a slab of finite width for every \(\gamma > 0\). They also state that they cannot take \(\gamma = 0\), which is what stops the argument short of the critical parameter.

The inequality removes the sprinkling. Conditioning on a shell surrounding a block fixes the states there and exposes a set \(A\) of open sites on the shell, each of which reaches the next block with probability close to one. Applying the inequality to \(A\) bounds the probability of reaching the next block from the origin, at the parameter \(p\) and with no additional sites opened. Iterating gives percolation in a slab of finite width, at \(p\) or below it.

Either form of the reduction closes the argument. Suppose the reduction gives a slab percolating at some \(q < p\), and take \(p = p_c\). A slab lies in \(\mathbb{Z}^d\), so \(\theta(q) > 0\) with \(q < p_c\), contradicting the definition of \(p_c\). Suppose instead the reduction gives a slab percolating at \(p\) itself. A slab lies in a half-space, and two theorems then apply: Barsky, Grimmett and Newman prove that the percolation probability of a half-space vanishes at the critical density of the half-space, for bond and site percolation alike, and Grimmett and Marstrand identify that density with \(p_c\) for \(d \ge 3\).

The formalization

Everything above is formalized in Lean 4 with Mathlib, except the two statements named below. The model, the connection events, the percolation probability and the critical parameter are definitions in the files. The critical parameter is proved to lie in \([0,1]\), so \(\theta(p_c)\) is defined and the conclusion needs no side condition.

So are the supporting results. Conditioning on the entire cluster of a set factorizes exactly, two disjoint clusters factorize in either order, and a probability expands as a finite sum over the possible clusters. The percolation probability is monotone in the parameter and in the underlying graph, vanishes below \(p_c\), and \(0 < p_c < 1\). An event depending on \(n\) sites has probability Lipschitz in the parameter with constant \(n\). An exploration whose every step succeeds with probability at least \(a\), after any history, reaches an upward-closed target at least as often as an independent Bernoulli \(a\) sequence. The example showing that the second inclusion above can be strict is machine-checked.

Status

Two statements remain unproved, of which only one is a conjecture.

The displayed inequality is the conjecture. For labels incident to two sites it is a theorem, proved in Anthropic's formalization.

The slab reduction is not a conjecture. Kozma and Nitzan derive it from the inequality, and for bond percolation that derivation is formalized here in some fifteen thousand lines; for site percolation it has to be carried out again. It also requires two estimates at a supercritical parameter, uniqueness of the large cluster in a finite box and the hitting of each face of a box, which are finite-volume forms of the Burton and Keane theorem and are not formalized here.

All of these are named hypotheses in the files, so every statement depending on them displays them, and the reduction of the problem to them is itself machine-checked. Everything else on this page is proved, with no unfinished step and with only the three axioms Lean provides.
